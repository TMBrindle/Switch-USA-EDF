Interconnection headroom curves for ReEDS zones

A rising cost curve for network-upgrade headroom in each of the 134 ReEDS zones, estimated from LBNL project-level interconnection costs and built into Switch. The aim is to test interconnection policy as changes to that curve.

Owner: Tom BrindleStarted: 30 Sept 2026Repo: Switch-USA-EDF, branch tom/interconnection-headroom

Why this project

For new wind and solar, the binding transmission constraint over the next five years is mostly not interregional backbone. It is headroom on the existing 69–345 kV network a few hops from the point of interconnection. Under participant funding, a shortage of that headroom shows up as network-upgrade charges assigned to individual projects. These costs have risen sharply in PJM, MISO and SPP, and they are the main grid-side reason projects withdraw from queues.

Zonal capacity expansion models mostly miss this. In Switch-USA-PG-ReEDS, the network cost a new solar MW pays does not depend on how much has already been built nearby. So the model can't show a zone filling up, and it can't value reforms that make headroom cheaper or larger.

What exists already

  • ReEDS and reV trace a path along existing lines from each site's point of interconnection to the zone centre, priced at 50% of greenfield cost to stand in for reconductoring. ReEDS also applies a flat $100/kW intra-zone adder to net capacity additions, calibrated to historical gas interconnection costs. Both are fixed per site and don't rise with cumulative builds. PowerGenome passes a similar distance-based reinforcement cost into Switch.
  • LBNL has extracted project-level interconnection costs from study PDFs for MISO, PJM, SPP, NYISO, ISO-NE, CAISO and a set of non-ISO balancing areas. The costs are split into point-of-interconnection and network-upgrade components. Projects with high network-upgrade costs cluster geographically, and capacity (NRIS) service needs more network than energy-only service.
  • ReEDS also limits near-term builds with county-level queue volumes (interconnection_queues.csv). That is a quantity cap, not a cost curve.

I haven't found a published zone-level network-upgrade curve that rises with saturation and is built into a US capacity expansion model. Ask the LBNL interconnection team before going further.

Method

1. Saturation by zone and year

For each zone z and year t:

saturation(z,t) = [ Σ_tech w_tech × MW added since 2010
                    − 0.8 × Σ_tech w_tech × MW retired since 2010 ]⁺  ÷  headroom proxy(z)

Every new generator uses headroom, including gas. The weight w is the share of the network one MW of nameplate occupies under interconnection-study conditions, with solar as the reference at 1.0. The starting weights are wind 0.75 (deliverability studies usually credit its geographic diversity), storage 0.5 (charging can relieve the network as well as load it), and gas and everything else 1.0. A retiring plant frees headroom at its own weight.

The default headroom proxy is the zone's AC transfer capacity: the sum of NARIS 2024 county-to-county limits inside the zone plus half of those across its boundary. The alternative proxy is all nameplate capacity online in 2010. fit-weights searches over the proxy, the wind, storage and gas weights, and the retirement-reuse share (0, 0.5 or 0.8), and ranks every combination by fit to the LBNL costs. Weights are identified only where zones differ in technology mix, so combinations within about 2 AIC of the best should be treated as equally supported.

Headroom use versus cost per MW

The model keeps two effects separate. The weights set how fast each technology fills a zone. The regression's technology dummies set what each technology pays per MW at a given saturation, which mostly reflects siting: gas tends to reuse brownfield sites near load and pipelines, while wind and solar sit where the resource is. Before this change, gas had a weight of zero and so never filled a zone, which tilted Switch towards gas in constrained zones.

2. Cost regression

log(1 + NU $/kW) = spline(saturation) + tech + ERIS/NRIS + β·log(MW)
                   + γ·(queue year − 2015) + planning-regime FE + ε

Each project gets its zone's saturation in the year it entered the queue. The central sample is active and completed projects. Withdrawn projects overstate costs because they were studied in overloaded clusters, so they are a high-cost sensitivity. Standard errors are clustered by zone. A spline knot is kept only if at least 50 projects sit above it. Beyond the 98th percentile of observed saturation, the last slope is extended and never allowed to turn negative. Tranches priced on that extrapolation are flagged.

3. Tranches

Each zone's curve starts at its 2026 saturation: the last EIA year plus planned additions and announced retirements. It is then split into seven steps, at +5%, +10%, +15%, +25%, +25%, +50% and +100% of the headroom proxy. Each step is priced for a reference project (150 MW solar, NRIS, 2024 queue year) under the zone's regime effect. Prices are forced to be non-decreasing, so the curve is convex and the LP fills it in order. Zones whose regime isn't in the LBNL sample (SERTP, FRCC, NorthernGrid, WestConnect, ERCOT for now) get the mean regime effect.

PJM and NYISO still read low, which is implausible given PJM's upgrade costs. PJM added about 42 GW of gas and 19 GW of wind and solar since 2010, but it also retired about 60 GW, mostly coal. With a 0.8 reuse share, those retirements offset nearly all the additions. That fits much of PJM's new gas, which went to old plant sites, but it overstates the headroom available to solar that wants to connect elsewhere. It also ignores load growth using up headroom. The reuse variants in fit-weights test this directly. Expect to lower the reuse share, or apply it only to technologies that actually reuse retiring sites.

Switch formulation

The module is switch/study_modules/interconnection_headroom.py in Switch-USA-EDF; pg_to_switch.py writes its inputs when pg/settings/interconnection_headroom.yml is enabled.

Each ReEDS BA is an interconnection zone with explicit network capacity H, starting from the NARIS 2024 proxy H0. The cost curve is stored in units of fullness: step k is wk wide and costs ck per MW of generation. For each zone and period:

H                = H0 + MW of uprates built (GETs, reconductoring, new lines)
step k           ≤ w_k × H                          (uprates stretch every step)
released         ≤ s0 × MW of uprates built         (existing use fills less of a bigger network)

Σ w_g · new capacity of g   ≤   initial headroom
                              + reuse × Σ w_g × existing capacity retired or suspended
                              + Σ steps + released
  • All of this is linear, so the model stays an LP. The released term is exact for small uprates and slightly conservative for large ones; an exact version would need integer variables.
  • Each uprate option has a cap (share of H0), a cost per MW of network capacity and a first available year. Steps, released headroom and uprates are annualised over 40 years at the model interest rate.
  • Every new build uses headroom at its technology weight; distributed generation never does. Only NEW_GEN_BLD_YRS count, because existing capacity is already reflected in where each curve starts.
  • Under state or interconnect aggregation, each aggregate load zone keeps its BAs as separate interconnection zones and pools their headroom.

What it reports

  • ic_spend.csv: overnight and annual reinforcement spend by zone, period and type (reactive upgrades along the curve, GETs, reconductoring, new lines), with the network MW added and generation MW enabled.
  • ic_network.csv: base network capacity and the MW and % added. Uprate MW are model decisions. Reactive MW are an estimate: the curve records what generators paid, so the report divides that spend by an engineering cost of network capacity (a placeholder) and shows the implied network MW per generation MW as a sanity check.
  • ic_headroom.csv: headroom used, freed and bought per load zone, with the constraint dual.

On the Switch three-zone toy, North needed more room for wind than its cheap first step allowed. Instead of buying the $3M/MW second step, the model built 1 MW of GETs and 2.5 MW of reconductoring. That raised North's network capacity 35% and stretched the cheap step from 2.0 to 2.7 MW, and existing use filling less of the network released another 1.05 MW. Total system cost fell 1.2% against the run without uprate options.

Before running it in Switch-USA-PG, confirm that PowerGenome's reinforcement cost (tx_capex) is removed from wind and solar connection costs, keeping spur lines. pg_to_switch.py does this when the setting is on, and writes ic_connect_cost_check.csv to verify it.

Policy scenarios

Switch is a social planner, so changing who pays for upgrades has no effect on its own. Each scenario changes the curve's position, stretch, height or steepness.

referenceNoneEmpirical curve with each zone's regime effect. No uprate options: today's reinforcement is reactive and already embedded in the curve.
best_regimeHeightEvery zone gets the lowest regime effect, with the slope held fixed.
getsNetwork capacitySwitch may add GETs up to 10% of H0 at $20/kW of network.

proactive_planningNetwork capacity and steepnessAll options including new intra-zonal lines (up to 100% of H0 at $250/kW from 2032), and the rise of the curve scaled by 0.7.

All uprate costs, caps and dates, the slope multiplier and the reactive engineering cost are placeholders. Replace them from Brattle (GETs in SPP), the GridLab/Berkeley reconductoring study, the ReEDS cost surface and MISO/SPP cost guides, with caps from HIFLD line-km by voltage. The next estimation step is regime-specific slopes, so steepness can come from data instead of a multiplier.

Open issues

  • Data support. On synthetic data, 98% of projects had saturation below 0.16, so most tranches are extrapolated. If the real data look the same, the upper curve rests on the extrapolation rule. Options are a finer step grid, the generation proxy, or anchoring the top of the curve to withdrawn-project costs.
  • Endogenous proxy. NARIS 2024 limits include lines built because of IBR, such as CREZ. That lowers measured saturation exactly where development has been heaviest. A pre-2010 network snapshot would be cleaner if one exists.
  • Retirement reuse and load growth. See the PJM note above.
  • Regime coverage. ERCOT and the non-ISO Southeast and West rely on the mean regime effect unless LBNL's non-ISO release covers them. ERCOT uses connect-and-manage, so its cost appears as congestion rather than upgrade charges; the hub version below handles that properly.
  • Withdrawal selection. The completed sample is survivor-biased and the withdrawn sample is cluster-inflated. Report results under both.
